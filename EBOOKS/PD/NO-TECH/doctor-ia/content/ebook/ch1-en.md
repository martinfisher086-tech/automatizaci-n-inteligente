## Introduction: Why Naming What You Feel Matters More Than You Think

There's a question that seems simple and yet many of us answer with a shrug: How are you feeling?

"Bad." "Weird." "Stressed, I guess." "I don't know, something just doesn't feel right."

These responses are not character flaws. They are the result of something science has begun to understand much more clearly in recent years: many people — especially women who grew up in cultures where "I'm fine" is the socially acceptable answer — never learned a real emotional vocabulary.

Neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett has spent decades researching how the brain builds emotions. Her proposal, known as the constructed emotion theory, suggests something fascinating: emotions are not automatic reactions that "happen to you". They are, to a large extent, predictions that your brain elaborates based on past experiences, your body's state, and the context you're in. In simpler terms: your brain needs emotional concepts to be able to feel differentiated emotions. Without the word, the experience remains in a diffuse, generalized state that's difficult to manage.

This means something powerful: the more emotional words you have available, the more precisely your brain can construct — and regulate — what you're feeling. Barrett calls this "emotional granularity". People with high emotional granularity not only describe themselves with more nuance; they also handle stress more effectively, have better relationships, and report greater overall well-being.

Naming what you feel is not an intellectual exercise. It's a regulation tool.

This chapter is going to give you that tool.

## Section 1: Why Don't We Know What We Feel?

Before talking about solutions, there's something important I want you to hear: not knowing what you feel is not your fault.

There was a time — and in many families and cultures it still persists — where "difficult" emotions were quickly silenced. "Don't cry." "Don't get like that." "It's not that serious." "Smile, life is beautiful." These phrases, repeated throughout childhood, teach something very specific: that certain emotions are dangerous, unacceptable, or exaggerated. The learned solution is to suppress them — or at least, not talk about them.

The result, decades later, is that many adult women have developed what psychologists call subclinical alexithymia: a difficulty identifying and describing their own emotional states. It's not a serious diagnosis. It's simply a limited emotional vocabulary, a muscle that was never exercised.

Another factor: we live accelerated lives. Between work, household responsibilities, caring for others (children, parents, partners, friends), and digital hyperconnectivity, the time to truly ask yourself how you're feeling is scarce. The unnamed emotion transforms into shoulder tension, insomnia, that "something" you can't quite pinpoint but that weighs on you.

Clinical psychology research shows that people with limited emotional vocabulary tend to:

- Experience more diffuse and hard-to-manage distress
- Have greater difficulty asking for support (because they don't know exactly what they need)
- Respond more reactively to conflict
- Present more somatic symptoms (tension, headaches, digestive problems) as an expression of unprocessed emotions

The good news is that this can be changed. Emotional vocabulary is learned. And like any skill, it improves with practice.

## Section 2: The Emotion Wheel — Your New Inner Map

In 1980, psychologist Robert Plutchik proposed that human emotions are organized into families, like colors in a wheel. There are eight primary emotions (joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, anticipation, anger, and disgust), from which hundreds of more complex emotions are mixed and graduated.

You can think of the Emotion Wheel like a map. When you say "I feel bad", it's like saying "I'm somewhere in Latin America" — it's true, but it doesn't help you know where you are or how to get where you want to go. The more specific you are, the easier it is to navigate.

Below you'll find more than 40 emotional words to start building your vocabulary. The first ones are the most well-known; the following are those most often confused or overlooked.

### Essential Emotional Vocabulary (40+ words)

**Joy Family:**
Contented, happy, euphoric, grateful, hopeful, enthusiastic, proud, relieved, satisfied, blissful

**Sadness Family:**
Sad, melancholic, heavy-hearted, desolate, nostalgic, disappointed, downcast, disconsolate

**Fear Family:**
Anxious, scared, anguished, restless, nervous, apprehensive, terrified, insecure

**Anger Family:**
Angry, frustrated, irritated, indignant, resentful, furious, exasperated

**Surprise Family:**
Surprised, bewildered, confused, astonished, impressed

**Disgust Family:**
Disgusted, repulsed, ashamed, horrified

**Complex Emotions:**
Ambivalent, vulnerable, emotionally exhausted, overwhelmed, disconnected, numbed, guilty, jealous, envious, lonely (even when surrounded by people), resigned

### 10 Emotions Worth Knowing Better

**1. Melancholy vs. Sadness**
Sadness is a direct response to a specific loss or disappointment. Melancholy is more diffuse: it's a sweet and somewhat nostalgic sadness, sometimes without a clear cause. You might feel melancholic while listening to an old song or on a rainy afternoon for no apparent reason.

**2. Anguish vs. Anxiety**
Anxiety tends to be future-oriented ("What if...?"). Anguish is more physical, more present, almost oppressive in the chest. Many women describe anguish as "a weight I can't explain where it comes from".

**3. Frustration vs. Anger**
Anger arises when something hurts or attacks us. Frustration appears when we try something and encounter an obstacle. They're relatives, but they have different causes — and different solutions.

**4. Indignation vs. Anger**
Indignation has a moral component: it activates when we perceive an injustice, not just towards ourselves but towards other people. It's anger with conviction.

**5. Emotional Exhaustion vs. Physical Tiredness**
You can have slept eight hours and still feel emotionally exhausted. This type of exhaustion comes from giving too much of yourself — caring, containing, mediating — without receiving equivalent support.

**6. Ambivalence**
Feeling two contradictory things at the same time and with equal intensity. You can love someone and simultaneously need distance. You can be grateful and resentful. Ambivalence is not confusion — it's normal human complexity.

**7. Disconnection / Emotional Numbness**
Sometimes, when emotions are too intense for too long, the nervous system "turns down the volume". You don't feel sadness or joy or anything clearly. This isn't indifference — it's a form of protection that deserves attention.

**8. Loneliness vs. Being Alone**
Being alone is a physical condition. Loneliness is an emotion you can feel surrounded by people, in a full meeting, in a relationship. It's the feeling of not being truly seen or understood.

**9. Resignation vs. Acceptance**
Acceptance is active: recognizing reality as it is and finding peace with it. Resignation is passive and painful: surrendering because you see no way out. They might look similar from the outside, but they feel completely different inside.

**10. Shame vs. Guilt**
Guilt says "I did something bad". Shame says "I am something bad". This distinction, widely studied by Brené Brown, is crucial: guilt can motivate changes; shame tends to paralyze us.

## Section 3: The 3-Minute Daily Exercise

Now we'll move to practice. This exercise is simple, requires nothing special, and has evidence behind it: the mere act of labeling emotions reduces amygdala activation (the brain's alarm center) and activates the prefrontal cortex, which is the region responsible for regulation and reasoning. In everyday words: naming what you feel helps you calm down.

The exercise has two moments: a morning review and an evening review. Each takes 90 seconds.

### Morning Check-in (upon waking or with your first drink of the day)

Before checking your phone, before thinking about what you have to do, give yourself a moment. Close your eyes or lower your gaze. Take three slow breaths.

Then ask yourself:

**What's happening in my body right now?**
(Tension, lightness, heaviness, energy, calm, agitation — note physical sensations first)

**What name would I give this?**
Use the vocabulary from this chapter. Don't look for the "correct" answer. Look for the most honest one.

**Is there something specific coloring my morning?**
(A pending conversation, something you're anticipating with joy or dread, something you left unresolved yesterday)

Write one or two words. You don't need a paragraph. "Anxious about the 3 PM meeting" is enough.

### Evening Check-in (before sleeping or after dinner)

This moment is for review, not judgment.

**What was the most intense emotion I felt today?**
It doesn't have to be the most dramatic. It can be a subtle joy or a quick irritation.

**What triggered it?**
A situation, a person, a memory, a piece of news.

**How did I respond to that emotion?**
Did you express it, suppress it, divert it? Without judgment. Just observation.

**Is there anything you want to process before sleeping or that you can let go of today?**

Again, write two or three lines. No more. The goal is not to solve — it's to notice.

### Why This Exercise Works

The simple act of naming an emotion — what researchers call "affect labeling" — has been shown in multiple studies to reduce the intensity of the emotional experience. It doesn't eliminate it, but makes it more manageable. It's like turning down the volume of a song that was too loud: the song continues, but now you can think.

Over time, this habit trains your brain to develop the emotional granularity we mentioned at the beginning. Weeks after practicing it, many people report that they no longer reach the end of the day without knowing why they're exhausted or irritated. They start to notice emotions in time, before they accumulate.

That's exactly what we want to build.

## Key Points

- **Naming emotions is a regulation tool, not an intellectual exercise.** The more precisely you identify what you're feeling, the easier it is to manage.
- **Not knowing what you feel is not your fault.** Emotional vocabulary is learned, and culture, family, and modern life's rhythm have limited that learning for many of us.
- **Three minutes a day are enough to start.** The morning and evening check-ins activate the prefrontal cortex and reduce emotional reactivity over time.

## Your Turn

Before moving to the next chapter, I propose this writing exercise. You don't need to do it perfectly. You just need to do it.

**Exercise: Your First Emotional Map**

Take a notebook or open a note on your phone. Answer these questions honestly:

1. What are the three emotions you feel most frequently? (You can use the list in this chapter to find the exact words)
2. Is there any emotion on the list that you recognized as "I feel this often but never knew how to name it"?
3. Is there any emotion you've been avoiding naming? Why do you think that is?
4. When was the last time someone asked you how you were feeling truly — and you had space to respond honestly?

There are no correct answers. This map is yours, and it's the starting point of everything that comes in this book.
